PCM reference

The equations, terms, symbols and settings used across the three PCM steps, in one place. Keep it open next to the simulator, or use it to look something up when a word is new. Everything about the array itself (Ohm’s law, Kirchhoff, converters, IR drop) is the same as for ReRAM. What changes is the cell: how a pulse moves it, how a weight is written, and what happens afterwards.

Learning path

Three short lessons, each building on the one before. If you are new to this, go in order.

Step 1

PCM vs ReRAM

A glass-and-crystal cell, heated by current pulses. You will SET, RESET and read one, and see how it differs from a ReRAM cell, including drift.

 Step 2

How a weight gets written

From a number to a conductance: choose the cell, round to a level, RESET, then climb with SET pulses and read back. An overshoot means starting again.

 Step 3

The crossbar simulator

Put cells in a grid and multiply a vector by a matrix in one read, then age the array and watch the answers drift.

The signal chain in one line

Everything in the simulator sits somewhere on this path. The middle is analog. The two ends are converters to and from the digital world.

  1. digital

    Input x

    0 to 1

    A number from the model or the previous layer.

  2. converter

    DAC

    V = x × V_read

    Rounds the input and makes a voltage.

  3. analog

    Word lines

    V_1 … V_M

    One voltage per row, all applied at once.

  4. analog

    Cells

    I = V × G

    Each cell stores a weight as G and multiplies.

  5. analog

    Bit lines

    I = Σ V × G

    The currents of a column add on the wire.

  6. analog

    Subtract

    I⁺ − I⁻

    The pair gives a signed result.

  7. converter

    ADC

    code → y

    Turns the current into an integer, then a number.

Key equations

Each one has a worked example with the simulator's default numbers (Gmin = 0.5 µS, Gmax = 50 µS) and a pointer to where you can see it. Equations 8, 9, 10, 12 and 13 describe this teaching model, not a measured device.

1. Ohm's law, one cell

I = V × G      (G = 1 / R)

The current through a cell is the voltage across it times its conductance. This is the multiplication.

Example. 0.2 V × 50 µS = 10 µA (the same cell is 20 kΩ).

Used in: Step 1 (read a cell). Step 3, every cell of the array.

2. Kirchhoff's current law, one bit line

I_j = Σ_i  V_i × G_ij

All cells on a bit line feed one wire, and the currents simply add. This is the accumulation.

Example. 0.10 V × 60 µS + 0.20 V × 30 µS = 6 + 6 = 12 µA.

Used in: Step 3, crossbar step 4 (Sum columns).

3. Input to voltage (DAC)

V_i = x_i × V_read

An input between 0 and 1 is rounded to one of 2^b − 1 steps and turned into a word-line voltage no larger than the read voltage.

Example. x = 0.5 and V_read = 0.2 V give 0.10 V. With a 4-bit DAC the steps are 1/15 apart.

Used in: Step 3, Input DAC slider.

4. Weight to conductance

level = round(|w| × (L − 1))
G = Gmin + level / (L − 1) × (Gmax − Gmin)
L = 2^bits

The weight magnitude is rounded to one of L levels and mapped linearly onto the window between Gmin and Gmax. The sign picks the G⁺ or G⁻ cell.

Example. |w| = 0.62 with 3 bits: level = round(4.34) = 4, so G = 0.5 + 4/7 × 49.5 = 28.8 µS.

Used in: Step 2, step 3 (Round to a level). Step 3, Weight levels slider.

5. Differential read-out

y = (I⁺ − I⁻) / (V_read × (Gmax − Gmin))

Subtract the two bit-line currents of a pair and divide by the scale factor to turn current back into a number. Gmin is in both cells and cancels.

Example. x = 0.5, G⁺ = 28.8 µS, G⁻ = 0.5 µS: I⁺ = 2.88 µA, I⁻ = 0.05 µA, so y = 2.83 / 9.9 = 0.286 (the ideal for the rounded weight is 0.5 × 0.571 = 0.286).

Used in: Step 2, step 6 table. Step 3, results table.

5b. Two other ways to store the sign

G⁺ only + reference column:
  level = round((w + 1)/2 × L),   G = Gmin + level/L × (Gmax − Gmin)
  G_ref = Gmin + (Gmax − Gmin)/2   (every row of the reference column)
  y = 2 × (I − I_ref) / (V_read × (Gmax − Gmin))

Unsigned, negatives clipped:
  level = round(max(w, 0) × L),   G = Gmin + level/L × (Gmax − Gmin)
  y = I / (V_read × (Gmax − Gmin))

The differential pair spends two cells per weight. These two spend one. With a reference column the weight is shifted into 0 to 1 and the middle conductance stands for zero, so negatives work and the Gmin leakage cancels, but one set of levels covers both signs, so each step in w is twice as coarse. With no subtraction at all, negative weights are lost and the leakage of every cell stays in the sum.

Example. x = 0.5 (V = 0.1 V), w = 0.6, L = 15, Gmin = 0.5 µS, Gmax = 50 µS. Reference column: level 12, G = 40.1 µS, I = 4.01 µA, I_ref = 0.1 × 25.25 = 2.525 µA, so y = 2 × 1.485 / 9.9 = 0.300, exactly x × w. Unsigned: level 9, G = 30.2 µS, I = 3.02 µA, so y = 3.02 / 9.9 = 0.305. The extra 0.005 is the leakage x × Gmin/(Gmax − Gmin).

Used in: Step 3, Weight sign setting.

6. Output ADC

step = R / (2^(b−1) − 1)
R = rows × ADC range

The converter has 2^(b−1) − 1 steps on each side of zero between −R and +R. Smaller R gives finer steps, but any output larger than R is clipped. (Default analog layout.)

Example. 4 rows, 100% range, 8 bits: R = 4, step = 4 / 127 = 0.031.

Used in: Step 3, Output ADC and ADC range sliders.

7. The write loop

a_target = ln(G_target / Gmax) / ln(Gmin / Gmax)
pick V_n: the strongest SET whose nominal shrink factor f(V) is still ≥ a_target / a_n
if |G − G_target| ≤ tol × (Gmax − Gmin): stop
if G > G_target + tol: RESET (a = 1) and climb again

A SET only shrinks the amorphous dome, so the controller aims at the dome size a_target that gives the wanted conductance and chooses each pulse so that it should not shrink the dome past it. A strong pulse is used far away and a gentle one close by. After every pulse the cell is read. If a pulse overshoots, nothing can undo it: the cell is RESET and the climb starts again.

Example. G_target = 28.8 µS gives a_target = 0.12. From the erased cell (a = 1) a pulse of about 1.75 V takes a to about 0.29 (G from 0.5 to 13 µS), then about 1.55 V takes it to 0.13 (G about 28 µS). That is inside a ±2% tolerance of ± 1 µS, after two pulses.

Used in: Step 2, step 5. Step 3, Programming panel.

8. How a pulse moves the cell (teaching model)

G(a) = Gmax × (Gmin / Gmax)^a             a = amorphous fraction, 0 to 1
T_peak = 300 + 162 × V² × (1 − e^(−t_p / 30 ns))   K
rate r = exp(−((T_peak − 700) / 130)²)   for 430 K < T_peak < 900 K, else 0
SET:   a ← a × exp(−r × t_p × f / 40 ns)
RESET: T_peak > 900 K melts the cell and the fast edge freezes it: a ← 1

The pulse heats the cell. Between 430 K and 900 K it crystallizes, fastest near 700 K, so SET shrinks the amorphous dome a bit more each pulse. Above 900 K it melts, and a fast, short pulse quenches it into glass: a full dome and G = Gmin. G falls exponentially with dome size, so equal steps of G need unequal steps of a. f is a random factor near 1, set by σ, partly fixed per cell and partly new each pulse. This is an illustrative rule, not a measured device equation.

Example. V = 1.3 V and t_p = 50 ns: T_peak = 300 + 162 × 1.69 × 0.811 = 522 K, r = 0.153, so a shrinks by exp(−0.153 × 50 / 40) = 0.826 per pulse. From a = 1 the cell moves from 0.5 µS to 1.1 µS. A 3.1 V, 20 ns pulse reaches about 1060 K and RESETs it.

Used in: Step 1, Step 2, Step 3 Programming panel.

9. Time and energy of a write (teaching model)

SET pulse:    E = V² × t_p / R_h
RESET pulse:  E = (3.1 V)² × 20 ns / R_h
Verify read:  E = V_read² × G × t_read + E_ADC
(R_h = 5 kΩ heater)

Units work out as volts² / kΩ × ns = picojoules. Time is t_p per SET, 20 ns per RESET and (t_read + t_ADC) per verify read. Cells in one row are written in parallel, so a row takes as long as its slowest cell.

Example. One 1.3 V SET pulse of 50 ns: 1.69 × 50 / 5 = 16.9 pJ. One RESET: 9.61 × 20 / 5 = 38.4 pJ, more than two SET pulses.

Used in: Step 2, step 6 tiles. Step 3, Programming panel tiles.

10. Compute energy and break-even reuse (teaching model)

E_crossbar = Σ V × I × t_read
           + inputs × E_DAC + columns × E_ADC
E_digital  = (M × N) × (E_MAC + E_fetch)
break-even = E_write / (E_digital − E_crossbar)

The crossbar pays once to write its weights and then a small amount per product. The digital processor pays to fetch every weight on every product. The crossbar only wins on energy after enough products reuse the same weights. The constants in the efficiency panel are ballpark values you can edit.

Example. If writing the weights costs 1,400 pJ and each product saves 15 pJ, the break-even is about 93 products.

Used in: Step 3, efficiency panel.

11. IR drop

solved numerically (Gauss-Seidel)

There is no short formula. Every wire segment has a resistance R_w, and the simulator solves the whole network of cells and wires. Cells far from the drivers see less than the intended voltage, so their currents come out lower than the ideal. Larger conductances draw more current, so they make the drop worse.

Example. With 200 Ω segments and default settings the RMS output error grows from 0.032 (Gmax = 20 µS) to 0.056 (50 µS) and 0.090 (100 µS).

Used in: Step 3, Wire R slider.

12. Drift after the write

G(t) = G(1 s) × t^(−ν)
ν = 0.005 + 0.095 × a

After the write the glass relaxes and the conductance falls as a power law of the time t since the write (in seconds, with G(1 s) the value right after the write). The exponent ν grows with the size of the amorphous dome: nearly crystalline cells barely move, mostly amorphous ones drift much more. The verify read happens within the first second, so it cannot see any of this.

Example. G = 28.8 µS has a = 0.12, so ν = 0.016. After a year (3.15 × 10⁷ s) it reads 0.016 → 28.8 × 0.75 = 21.7 µS. The erased partner cell (a = 1, ν = 0.1) falls from 0.50 to 0.09 µS.

Used in: Step 1, drift slider. Step 2, step 6. Step 3, Time since write slider.

13. A single global gain against drift

y_corrected = y × t^ν_ref        (ν_ref = 0.03 in the simulator)

A chip can multiply all outputs by one factor that grows with time. It is exact only for cells whose ν equals ν_ref. Cells with a smaller ν are over-corrected and cells with a larger ν are under-corrected, and every cell in an array has its own ν.

Example. After a year the gain is 3.15 × 10⁷ ^ 0.03 = 1.68. The 28.8 µS cell (ν = 0.016) keeps only 0.75 of its value, so the gain overshoots to 0.75 × 1.68 = 1.27, 27% too high.

Used in: Step 3, Global drift gain setting.

Glossary

Type to filter the 48 terms by name or by anything in the definition.

Adaptive pulse
A write scheme where the controller picks each SET amplitude from how far the cell still has to go: strong pulses far away, gentle ones close by. The default write method in the simulator.
ADC (analog-to-digital converter)
Turns a bit-line current into an integer code. More bits give finer output steps. In the efficiency model each extra bit doubles the ADC's energy.
Amorphous and crystalline
Glass-like and ordered. The amorphous state resists current (low G) and the crystalline state conducts well (high G). The cell holds a mix of the two.
Amorphous dome, fraction a
The glassy cap over the heater in a mushroom cell. In this model its size is a, from 0 (no glass) to 1 (full dome), and G = Gmax × (Gmin / Gmax)^a.
Bit line (BL)
The wire that runs down a column and collects the currents of every cell in it. Its total current is the sum of V × G over the column.
Bit slicing (binary weighted encoding)
Storing each bit of a weight in its own pair of two-state cells and combining the bit planes with shifts and adds in digital logic. It uses more cells and more ADC conversions, but each cell only needs two well-separated states. The Cell encoding menu in Step 3 offers it.
Block erase
A RESET applied to a block of cells so that all of them start from the full-dome state, Gmin, before the SET climb begins.
Chalcogenide (GST)
The family of alloys used for PCM, for example germanium-antimony-tellurium (Ge₂Sb₂Te₅). They switch between glass and crystal in nanoseconds, which is why they are also used in rewritable optical discs.
Compute-in-memory (in-memory computing)
Doing arithmetic inside the memory array where the data is stored, instead of moving the data to a separate processor.
Conductance (G)
How easily a cell passes current. G = 1 / R, measured in siemens (S). The cells here sit between 0.5 and 50 µS, which is 2 MΩ down to 20 kΩ. Conductances on a shared wire simply add, which is why weights are stored as conductance and not as resistance.
Crossbar
A grid of word lines crossing bit lines, with one memory cell at every crossing.
Crystallization
SET: a longer, gentler pulse keeps the cell hot but below melting (best near 700 K here), so the glass slowly turns back into crystal and the dome shrinks. Each pulse shrinks it a bit more.
Cycle-to-cycle variation
The same cell responds a little differently to the same pulse each time it is written.
DAC (digital-to-analog converter)
Turns an input number into a voltage on a word line. The simulator rounds inputs to 2^b − 1 steps.
Device-to-device variation
Different cells respond differently to the same pulse, because no two domes form identically. The σ slider sets how large the spread is.
Differential pair (G⁺ / G⁻)
Two cells that together store one signed weight. The positive part goes in G⁺, the negative part in G⁻, and the read-out subtracts the two currents.
Drift compensation
Correcting for drift in the readout, for example with a single gain that grows with time. It works only as well as the cells share one ν.
Drift exponent (ν)
The power in the drift law. In this model ν = 0.005 + 0.095 × a, so nearly crystalline cells (small dome) drift little and mostly amorphous cells drift a lot.
Endurance
How many SET and RESET cycles a cell survives before its window shrinks or it stops switching. Step 1 has a write-cycles slider that shows it.
High-resistance state (HRS) and low-resistance state (LRS)
The two extremes of a cell. HRS is the lowest conductance (Gmin, full amorphous dome) and LRS is the highest (Gmax, no dome). Between them the cell can hold intermediate analog levels.
IR drop
Voltage lost along the resistance of the wires. Cells far from the drivers see less voltage, so their currents are lower than the ideal. The Wire R slider controls it and the simulator solves for it.
Joule heating
Heat from the current passing through the cell. PCM is programmed by it, so SET and RESET pulses are heat pulses as much as voltage pulses.
Kirchhoff's current law
The currents flowing into a point add up to the currents flowing out. It is why all the cell currents on one bit line sum into a single current.
Level (conductance level)
One of the 2^b discrete conductance values a cell is aimed at. Level k of L − 1 steps sits at Gmin + k / (L − 1) × (Gmax − Gmin).
Matrix-vector multiplication (MVM)
y = xᵀW: every output is the dot product of the input vector with one column of weights. It is the main operation inside neural-network layers.
Melt-quench
RESET: a short, strong pulse heats the cell past the melting point (900 K in this model) and the pulse edge is so fast that the melt freezes into glass instead of crystallizing.
Multiply-accumulate (MAC)
One multiplication added to a running total. A matrix-vector product is many MACs.
Mushroom cell
A common PCM layout: a narrow heater electrode under a layer of phase-change material, so the hottest spot, and the glassy dome, forms over the heater.
Neural network (NN)
A model built from layers. Each layer computes weighted sums of its inputs and then applies a simple nonlinear function. The weights are numbers learned during training. These lessons only need the weighted sum, which a crossbar computes in one read.
Non-volatile
Keeps its stored state with the power off.
Ohm's law
V = I × R, or equivalently I = V × G. It is the multiplication step in the crossbar.
On/off ratio (window)
Gmax / Gmin. With the defaults, 50 / 0.5 = 100. A wider window leaves more room between levels.
Open-loop write
Applying a fixed train of pulses without reading the cell back. It is cheap, but device variation lands directly in the stored weight.
Overshoot
A SET pulse that leaves the conductance above the target band. PCM has no gentle step down, so the cell must be RESET and written again.
Phase-change memory (PCM)
A memory cell made of a chalcogenide alloy that can be glass (amorphous, high resistance) or crystal (low resistance). Heat pulses switch it, and it keeps its state with the power off. It can hold a bit or, with intermediate dome sizes, a neural-network weight.
Program-and-verify (write-verify)
Writing in a loop: pulse, read back, compare with the target, and repeat until the cell is within tolerance or the pulses run out. In PCM an overshoot cannot be undone by another SET, so it costs a RESET and a new climb.
Quantization
Rounding to one of a limited set of values: inputs by the DAC, weights to conductance levels, outputs by the ADC.
Read disturb
A read at too high a voltage can heat the cell enough to change its state, so the read voltage is kept low.
Reference column
One extra column of cells, all at the middle conductance Gmid, shared by every output. Its current is subtracted from each output, so a single cell per weight can still stand for a negative weight.
RESET
A pulse that melts the cell and quenches it into glass. In this model it always returns the cell to the full dome and Gmin, so it is both the erase and the only way down.
Resistance drift (drift)
After the write the amorphous glass slowly relaxes and the conductance falls as a power law of time, G(t) = G(1 s) × t^(−ν). Steps 1, 2 and 3 each have a time slider that shows it.
SET
A pulse that crystallizes part of the dome and so raises the conductance. In PCM it comes in small steps and can only go up.
Sneak path
In a passive crossbar, current that finds a route through other cells instead of the selected one, which corrupts reads and writes. Transistors (1T1R) or selector devices suppress it. This simulator does not model sneak paths.
Tolerance
How close to the target counts as done in a verify loop, set as a percent of the Gmax − Gmin window.
Unsigned encoding (negatives clipped)
One cell per weight and no subtraction. A cell cannot be negative, so negative weights are stored as 0, and the Gmin leakage of every cell adds a small offset to each output.
Von Neumann bottleneck
The time and energy spent moving data between a separate memory and processor. Computing in the array avoids moving the weights.
Weight
A number the model learned. Here it runs from −1 to +1 and is stored as conductance in one or two cells.
Word line (WL)
The wire that runs along a row and carries one input voltage to every cell in that row.

No term matches. Try a shorter word.

What each setting does

Each row says what happens when you raise the setting. The numbers are the RMS output error (or the write cost) measured on the simulator’s default 4 × 4 example with 10% variation and adaptive program-and-verify writing, changing one setting at a time. They are a guide to the direction and size of each effect, and will shift if you change other settings or the simulator is updated.

Common questions

Why store a weight as conductance and not resistance?

Because currents on a shared wire add in terms of conductance: I = V × (G₁ + G₂ + ...). Resistances in parallel combine through reciprocals, which does not give a sum. With conductance, the wire does the accumulation for free.

Why does a weight need two cells?

A conductance cannot be negative. One cell holds the positive part and one holds the negative part, and the read-out subtracts them. Step 2 walks through it.

Does reading a cell change the weight?

Not at a small read voltage: the read heats the cell far less than a pulse does. It does not stop the drift, though, which runs on its own clock from the moment of the write.

Is the crossbar analog or digital?

The array itself is analog: voltages go in, currents add, and a current comes out. The DACs and ADCs at the edges are the interface to the digital system around it.

Why can PCM only step up and not down?

SET crystallizes a little at a time, so a series of small pulses climbs gradually. Going the other way means melting the dome, and RESET melts and re-freezes the whole cell in one go. So there is no small step down: an overshoot costs a RESET and a new climb. That is why the write loop uses gentle pulses near the target.

Why verify after each pulse?

The same pulse shrinks the dome by a different factor in different cells. Reading back lets the controller stop each cell near its target, or RESET it if it overshot. In Step 3, switch to open loop and press Try a different cell to see the spread.

The weights were correct when I wrote them. Why do the outputs get worse later?

Drift. The glass in each cell relaxes and its conductance falls as a power law of time, by a factor that depends on how large the dome is. The verify read happens right after the pulse, so it cannot see this. Raise Time since write in Step 3 to watch it, and try the global gain to see what a single correction can and cannot do.

If PCM is so efficient, why does not every chip use it?

Writing is slow and costly (a RESET alone is tens of picojoules per cell), cells vary and drift, endurance is limited, large arrays suffer IR drop, and the converters take energy and area. It suits cases where the same weights are read many times, such as running a trained network. The efficiency panel in Step 3 lets you see where the advantage appears and where it does not.

Is this simulator a model of a real chip?

No. It is a generic teaching model. The device response, the energies and the timings are illustrative values, and it has not been compared against measured hardware or a circuit simulator such as SPICE.
