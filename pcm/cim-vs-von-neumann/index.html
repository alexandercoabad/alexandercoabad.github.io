PCM Compute-in-Memory vs Von Neumann

A neural-network layer is a matrix-vector product, y = xᵀW. A von Neumann processor has to carry every weight from a memory to its arithmetic units, a few at a time. A PCM crossbar keeps each weight as the conductance of a cell and does the whole product in a single read: the rows carry the inputs, every cell multiplies by Ohm’s law, and every column adds by Kirchhoff’s law. This page puts numbers on that advantage, shows where it comes from, and is honest about what it costs.

Overhead factor multiplies the crossbar’s read energy to stand in for routing, buffers, control and the digital work around the array. Products per write is how many products use the weights before they are written again; the write cost is spread over them. PCM weights drift, so the array must also be rewritten at the interval you choose (Rewrite every). The products it can serve between rewrites are the product rate times that interval, if that is smaller than Products per write.

3. What moves per matrix-vector product

A von Neumann machine keeps the weights in a memory and the arithmetic in a processor, joined by a channel of limited width. Every weight has to cross it every time it is used: this is the von Neumann bottleneck. A crossbar stores each weight as a conductance, so the weights stay put; only the inputs go in and only the outputs come out.

4. Compute power parallelism, TOPS and TOPS per watt

A processor is limited by whichever is slower: the arithmetic, or bringing the weights. A matrix-vector product has one MAC per weight, so it is usually limited by the memory, however many lanes the processor has. The crossbar has no weight traffic, and every cell works at once, so its compute rate is the number of weights divided by one short read. TOPS counts a multiply and an add as two operations per MAC, per second; TOPS per watt is the same count per unit of energy.

8. Drift: how long can the weights be trusted?

A PCM cell’s conductance slowly falls as its amorphous region relaxes, so the weights have to be corrected or rewritten from time to time. Rewriting costs write energy: this is the price paid for PCM’s large conductance window. The table shows what different rewrite intervals cost.

Why not use a crossbar for everything?

It only does a weighted sum, in low precision, on weights that stay put. Training changes the weights constantly. Attention layers multiply two activations, neither of which is a stored weight. Small matrices cannot pay for their converters. Sparse matrices waste cells.

Why not just batch more?

Batching works when many inputs are waiting. A single request, such as one sensor reading or one token, has batch 1. Batching also holds inputs until the batch fills, which adds delay.

What would make the processor win?

Weights in SRAM next to the lanes, a small matrix, a large batch, or a need for high precision. Try these in sections 1 and 2: the gap closes quickly.

These models leave out a lot: sneak paths, nonlinearity of the cells, converter area and speed limits, tile-to-tile communication, and everything a real chip needs around the array. Published chips report smaller gains than raw array numbers suggest for exactly these reasons.

Check yourself

A 1024 × 1024 matrix-vector product has about a million MACs. Why does a processor with thousands of lanes still not finish faster?

Because it has to bring a million weights to the lanes first, and each weight is used only once. The time is set by the memory bandwidth, not the number of lanes. In section 4, choose the GPU-style machine with batch 1 and watch the roofline: the point sits on the sloping part, far left of the flat ceiling.

Set the batch size to 1000. Who benefits?

The processor: its weight-fetch energy per product falls by the batch factor, and its point moves right on the roofline. The crossbar gains nothing from batching because it never fetched the weights. The gap shrinks, which is why a large-batch workload is the hard case for in-memory computing.

The crossbar has to be written first. When does that pay off?

When the weights are used for many products before they are replaced. Section 6 shows the write energy spread over the products and the break-even count, and section 8 shows how the write rate limits it.

The array read is cheap. Why does the crossbar’s energy not shrink to nothing?

Every input needs a DAC and every output needs an ADC (more than one when the matrix needs several tiles), and a real chip has routing and control around them. In section 6 the converters and overhead are most of the crossbar’s energy.

How does drift change the energy comparison?

Rewriting restores the weights, but each rewrite costs the whole write energy of the array. At a high product rate that cost is spread over a huge number of products and hardly matters; at a low rate it can dominate. Slide Rewrite every in section 2 and watch the share of rewriting in section 8. A drift correction postpones the rewrite, which is why the PCM crossbar simulator has a drift panel.
