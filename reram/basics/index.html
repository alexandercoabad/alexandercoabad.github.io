ReRAM vs an ordinary resistor

An ordinary resistor has a fixed value. A ReRAM cell is a resistor whose value you can change with electrical pulses, and which then keeps that value with the power off. Everything in the crossbar simulator follows from that one difference. This page lets you poke at it before you meet the array.

What a ReRAM cell is

A thin metal-oxide layer (for example HfOx) sits between two electrodes. A SET pulse builds a conductive filament of oxygen vacancies through the oxide, which lowers the resistance. A RESET pulse of opposite polarity or different amplitude breaks the filament, which raises it. A read at low voltage does not disturb the state, so within that read range the cell behaves like a resistor with whatever value was last written. That is the assumption behind the crossbar math in the simulator.

Think of a water pipe

A resistor is a pipe of fixed width: it passes a fixed amount of water for a given pressure. A ReRAM cell is a pipe whose width you can change with a strong push, and which stays at the new width after you let go.

Think of a lamp

A resistor is a fixed bulb. A ReRAM cell is a dimmer whose knob you turn with write pulses. It stays where you left it, even when the house power is cut.

Why computing cares

Conductance is the weight. Voltage is the input. Ohm's law (I = V·G) does the multiply, and the wire does the add. The weight is the physical state of the device, so it never has to be fetched.

Pulses below about 0.8 V do nothing (below the switching threshold). Stronger pulses move the filament further. Reading above about 0.8 V starts to behave like a weak SET pulse, which is called read disturb. The step size model is the same illustrative one the write panel in the simulator uses.

2. The I-V loop

A resistor draws a straight line: double the voltage, double the current, in both directions. A ReRAM cell draws a loop. Start in the high-resistance state, sweep the voltage up past the SET threshold, and the cell jumps to a low-resistance state and stays there on the way back. A negative sweep past the RESET threshold flips it back. The shaded band is the read range, where the cell is close to an ordinary resistor.

0.00 V

Drag the slider yourself and notice the order matters: the same voltage gives a different current depending on where you came from. That path dependence is the memory. Current is I = G(V)·V, with a small non-linearity added at higher voltage, so the cell is only roughly a resistor at low voltage.

3. Why it is not perfect

Sixteen cells were written to the same target. A resistor array would land within about 1% of the target. ReRAM cells scatter (variation), relax towards the middle as time passes (drift), and lose their separation after many write cycles (endurance). Move the sliders.

5. What you will meet in the simulator

Step 2 is the crossbar simulator. Each difference above explains one of its controls, so here is a preview of what to look for there.

The write panel

Erase, SET and RESET pulses, program-and-verify and the pulse-count map exist only because ReRAM must be programmed. A resistor array would skip all of it. Open the write panel.

The variation σ slider

The σ slider and the write-noise model represent ReRAM's imprecision (section 3 above). A resistor array would mainly have small, static mismatch. See it in the write map.

Gmin and Gmax

They represent the high-resistance and low-resistance states. A real resistor has one value, not two states, so a resistor array has no such range.

Weight levels

A ReRAM cell has to be written to one of several conductance levels in between its two extremes. A resistor array would need a different resistor value for every weight, fixed at manufacture.

Read voltage

The read voltage is kept small (0.2 V by default) so that the cell stays inside the band where it acts like a plain resistor and the crossbar math holds (section 2).

Efficiency panel

The weights never move: they are the devices. That is where the energy saving over a digital multiply-accumulate comes from. Open the efficiency panel.

6. Check yourself

Why can the simulator treat each cell as an ordinary resistor when computing?

Because the read voltage is small. In that range the read does not change the state and the I-V curve is close to linear, so each cell passes I = V·G with the G that was last written.

You power the chip off for a week and back on. What happens to the stored weights?

They are still there, because the filament state is physical and non-volatile. They may have drifted slightly (section 3), so a long-lived system may need to refresh or re-verify its weights.

Why does the simulator read back after every write pulse?

Pulse-to-pulse and device-to-device variation mean the same pulse moves different cells by different amounts. Reading the value back and adjusting is how the controller gets each cell within tolerance of its target.

A designer wants 6 bits per weight in one cell. Which slider in section 3 is the problem, and why?

Variation σ. Sixty-four levels packed between the two extremes are very close together, so cell-to-cell scatter makes neighbouring levels overlap. Fewer levels per cell tolerate more variation.
