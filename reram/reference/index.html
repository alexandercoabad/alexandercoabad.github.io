Learning path

Three short lessons, each building on the one before. If you are new to this, go in order.

Step 1

ReRAM vs an ordinary resistor

A cell is a resistor you can reprogram with pulses, and it keeps its value without power. You will SET, RESET and read one.

 Step 2

How a weight gets written

From a number to a conductance: choose the cell, round to a level, erase, then pulse and read back until it is close enough.

 Step 3

The crossbar simulator

Put cells in a grid. Voltages in, Ohm's law in every cell, currents added on the wires: a matrix multiply in one read.

The signal chain in one line

Everything in the simulator sits somewhere on this path. The middle is analog. The two ends are converters to and from the digital world.

  1. digital

    Input x

    0 to 1

    A number from the model or the previous layer.

  2. converter

    DAC

    V = x × V_read

    Rounds the input and makes a voltage.

  3. analog

    Word lines

    V_1 … V_M

    One voltage per row, all applied at once.

  4. analog

    Cells

    I = V × G

    Each cell stores a weight as G and multiplies.

  5. analog

    Bit lines

    I = Σ V × G

    The currents of a column add on the wire.

  6. analog

    Subtract

    I⁺ − I⁻

    The pair gives a signed result.

  7. converter

    ADC

    code → y

    Turns the current into an integer, then a number.

Key equations

Each one has a worked example with the simulator's default numbers and a pointer to where you can see it. Equations 8 to 10 describe this teaching model, not a measured device.

1. Ohm's law, one cell

I = V × G      (G = 1 / R)

The current through a cell is the voltage across it times its conductance. This is the multiplication.

Example. 0.2 V × 50 µS = 10 µA (the same cell is 20 kΩ).

Used in: Step 1 (read a cell). Step 2 trace table.

2. Kirchhoff's current law, one bit line

I_j = Σ_i  V_i × G_ij

All cells on a bit line feed one wire, and the currents simply add. This is the accumulation.

Example. 0.10 V × 60 µS + 0.20 V × 30 µS = 6 + 6 = 12 µA.

Used in: Step 2, walkthrough step 4 (Sum columns).

3. Input to voltage (DAC)

V_i = x_i × V_read

An input between 0 and 1 is rounded to one of 2^b − 1 steps and turned into a word-line voltage no larger than the read voltage.

Example. x = 0.5 and V_read = 0.2 V give 0.10 V. With a 4-bit DAC the steps are 1/15 apart.

Used in: Step 2, Input DAC slider.

4. Weight to conductance

level = round(|w| × (L − 1))
G = Gmin + level / (L − 1) × (Gmax − Gmin)
L = 2^bits

The weight magnitude is rounded to one of L levels and mapped linearly onto the window between Gmin and Gmax. The sign picks the G⁺ or G⁻ cell.

Example. |w| = 0.62 with 3 bits: level = round(4.34) = 4, so G = 5 + 4/7 × 95 = 59.3 µS.

Used in: Step 3, step 3 (Round to a level).

5. Differential read-out

y = (I⁺ − I⁻) / (V_read × (Gmax − Gmin))

Subtract the two bit-line currents of a pair and divide by the scale factor to turn current back into a number. Gmin is in both cells and cancels.

Example. x = 0.5, G⁺ = 59.3 µS, G⁻ = 5 µS: I⁺ = 5.93 µA, I⁻ = 0.50 µA, so y = 5.43 / 19 = 0.286 (the ideal for the rounded weight is 0.5 × 0.571 = 0.286).

Used in: Step 2, Result table. Step 3, step 6.

5b. Two other ways to store the sign

G⁺ only + reference column:
  level = round((w + 1)/2 × L),   G = Gmin + level/L × (Gmax − Gmin)
  G_ref = Gmin + (Gmax − Gmin)/2   (every row of the reference column)
  y = 2 × (I − I_ref) / (V_read × (Gmax − Gmin))

Unsigned, negatives clipped:
  level = round(max(w, 0) × L),   G = Gmin + level/L × (Gmax − Gmin)
  y = I / (V_read × (Gmax − Gmin))

The differential pair spends two cells per weight. These two spend one. With a reference column the weight is shifted into 0 to 1 and the middle conductance stands for zero, so negatives work and the Gmin leakage cancels, but one set of levels covers both signs, so each step in w is twice as coarse. With no subtraction at all, negative weights are lost and the leakage of every cell stays in the sum.

Example. x = 0.5 (V = 0.1 V), w = 0.6, L = 15, Gmin = 5 µS, Gmax = 100 µS. Reference column: level 12, G = 81 µS, I = 8.1 µA, I_ref = 0.1 × 52.5 = 5.25 µA, so y = 2 × 2.85 / 19 = 0.300, which is exactly x × w. Unsigned: level 9, G = 62 µS, I = 6.2 µA, so y = 6.2 / 19 = 0.326. The extra 0.026 is the leakage x × Gmin/(Gmax − Gmin).

Used in: Step 3, Weight sign setting and the worked example.

6. Output ADC

step = R / (2^(b−1) − 1)
R = rows × ADC range

The converter has 2^(b−1) − 1 steps on each side of zero between −R and +R. Smaller R gives finer steps, but any output larger than R is clipped. (Default analog layout.)

Example. 4 rows, 100% range, 8 bits: R = 4, step = 4 / 127 = 0.031.

Used in: Step 2, Output ADC and ADC range sliders.

7. The write loop

V_n = V_0 + n × ΔV
stop when  |G − G_target| ≤ tol × (Gmax − Gmin)

Each pulse in the same direction is a little stronger than the last. After every pulse the cell is read, and the loop ends when it is close enough or the pulse limit is reached. If a pulse overshoots, the loop switches to the other polarity and restarts from V_0.

Example. V_0 = 1.0 V and ΔV = 0.10 V give 1.0, 1.1, 1.2 V. A 2% tolerance on a 95 µS window is ± 1.9 µS.

Used in: Step 3, step 5. Step 2, write panel.

8. How a pulse moves the cell (teaching model)

drive = clamp((V − 0.8) / (2.4 − 0.8), 0, 1)
SET:    G ← G + 0.45 × drive × (Gmax − G) × f
RESET:  G ← G − 0.45 × drive × (G − Gmin) × f

Pulses below 0.8 V do nothing. Stronger pulses move G a larger fraction of the remaining distance toward Gmax (SET) or Gmin (RESET). f is a random factor near 1 whose spread is set by σ: part of it is fixed per cell and part changes each pulse. This is an illustrative rule, not a measured device equation.

Example. G = 5 µS and a 1.0 V SET: drive = 0.125, so G rises by about 0.45 × 0.125 × 95 = 5.3 µS (to about 10.3 µS when f = 1).

Used in: Step 1 and Step 3, step 5.

9. Time and energy of a write (teaching model)

SET pulse:    E = V × I_cc × t_p
RESET pulse:  E = V² × G × t_p
Verify read:  E = V_read² × G × t_read + E_ADC
(+ 0.5 pJ driver energy per pulse)

Units work out as volts × µA × ns = femtojoules. Time is t_p per pulse plus (t_read + t_ADC) per verify read. Cells in one row are written in parallel, so a row takes as long as its slowest cell.

Example. One 1.0 V SET pulse with I_cc = 50 µA and t_p = 50 ns: 1.0 × 50 × 50 = 2500 fJ = 2.5 pJ, plus 0.5 pJ, so 3.0 pJ.

Used in: Step 3, step 6. Step 2, write panel.

10. Compute energy and break-even reuse (teaching model)

E_crossbar = Σ V × I × t_read
           + inputs × E_DAC + columns × E_ADC
E_digital  = (M × N) × (E_MAC + E_fetch)
break-even = E_write / (E_digital − E_crossbar)

The crossbar pays once to write its weights and then a small amount per product. The digital processor pays to fetch every weight on every product. The crossbar only wins on energy after enough products reuse the same weights. The constants in the efficiency panel are ballpark values you can edit.

Example. If writing the weights costs 1,400 pJ and each product saves 15 pJ, the break-even is about 93 products.

Used in: Step 2, efficiency panel.

11. IR drop

solved numerically (Gauss-Seidel)

There is no short formula. Every wire segment has a resistance R_w, and the simulator solves the whole network of cells and wires. Cells far from the drivers see less than the intended voltage, so their currents come out lower than the ideal. Larger conductances draw more current, so they make the drop worse.

Example. With 200 Ω segments the output error in the default example grows from 0.070 (Gmax = 40 µS) to 0.164 (Gmax = 200 µS).

Used in: Step 2, Wire R slider.

Glossary

Type to filter the 38 terms by name or by anything in the definition.

1T1R
One access transistor in series with each cell. The transistor selects the cell to write and limits the current during SET. This simulator draws a passive crossbar with no transistors and has a separate SET compliance setting for the current limit.
ADC (analog-to-digital converter)
Turns a bit-line current into an integer code. More bits give finer output steps. In the efficiency model each extra bit doubles the ADC's energy.
Bit line (BL)
The wire that runs down a column and collects the currents of every cell in it. Its total current is the sum of V × G over the column.
Bit slicing (binary weighted encoding)
Storing each bit of a weight in its own pair of two-state cells and combining the bit planes with shifts and adds in digital logic. It uses more cells and more ADC conversions, but each cell only needs two well-separated states. The Cell encoding menu in Step 2 offers it.
Block erase
A single strong RESET pulse applied to a block of cells so that all of them start from the high-resistance state before writing.
Compute-in-memory (in-memory computing)
Doing arithmetic inside the memory array where the data is stored, instead of moving the data to a separate processor.
Conductance (G)
How easily a cell passes current. G = 1 / R, measured in siemens (S). The cells here sit between 5 and 100 µS, which is 200 kΩ down to 10 kΩ. Conductances on a shared wire simply add, which is why weights are stored as conductance and not as resistance.
Conductive filament
A thin channel of oxygen vacancies through the metal-oxide layer. A SET pulse builds or widens it and a RESET pulse dissolves part of it. Its size sets the cell's conductance.
Crossbar
A grid of word lines crossing bit lines, with one memory cell at every crossing.
Cycle-to-cycle variation
The same cell responds a little differently to the same pulse each time it is written.
DAC (digital-to-analog converter)
Turns an input number into a voltage on a word line. The simulator rounds inputs to 2^b − 1 steps.
Device-to-device variation
Different cells respond differently to the same pulse, because no two filaments form identically. The σ slider sets how large the spread is.
Differential pair (G⁺ / G⁻)
Two cells that together store one signed weight. The positive part goes in G⁺, the negative part in G⁻, and the read-out subtracts the two currents.
Reference column
One extra column of cells, all at the middle conductance Gmid, shared by every output. Its current is subtracted from each output, so a single cell per weight can still stand for a negative weight.
Unsigned encoding (negatives clipped)
One cell per weight and no subtraction. A cell cannot be negative, so negative weights are stored as 0, and the Gmin leakage of every cell adds a small offset to each output.
Drift (retention loss)
The stored conductance slowly wanders over time, even with the power off. Step 1 has a time slider that shows it.
Endurance
How many SET and RESET cycles a cell survives before its window shrinks or it stops switching. Step 1 has a write-cycles slider that shows it.
Forming
A one-time, higher-voltage step that creates the first filament in many oxide ReRAM cells. This teaching model leaves it out.
High-resistance state (HRS) and low-resistance state (LRS)
The two extremes of a cell. HRS is the lowest conductance (Gmin) and LRS is the highest (Gmax). Between them the cell can hold intermediate analog levels.
IR drop
Voltage lost along the resistance of the wires. Cells far from the drivers see less voltage, so their currents are lower than the ideal. The Wire R slider controls it and the simulator solves for it.
HfOx (hafnium oxide)
A common oxide for ReRAM. Hafnium dioxide (HfO₂) is also used as a thin gate insulator in advanced CMOS, so it fits standard chip processes. The x means the oxygen content is not fixed. Switching moves oxygen ions and builds a filament of oxygen vacancies. This simulator is generic and does not model HfOx specifically.
ISPP (incremental step pulse programming)
A write scheme where each new pulse is a little higher in amplitude than the last (V₀ + n × ΔV), with a read between pulses. The default write method in the simulator.
Kirchhoff's current law
The currents flowing into a point add up to the currents flowing out. It is why all the cell currents on one bit line sum into a single current.
Level (conductance level)
One of the 2^b discrete conductance values a cell is aimed at. Level k of L − 1 steps sits at Gmin + k / (L − 1) × (Gmax − Gmin).
Matrix-vector multiplication (MVM)
y = xᵀW: every output is the dot product of the input vector with one column of weights. It is the main operation inside neural-network layers.
Memristor
A resistor whose value depends on the history of the current through it. Leon Chua proposed the idea in 1971, and ReRAM cells are often described as memristive devices.
Multiply-accumulate (MAC)
One multiplication added to a running total. A matrix-vector product is many MACs.
Non-volatile
Keeps its stored state with the power off.
Ohm's law
V = I × R, or equivalently I = V × G. It is the multiplication step in the crossbar.
On/off ratio (window)
Gmax / Gmin. With the defaults, 100 / 5 = 20. A wider window leaves more room between levels.
Open-loop write
Applying a fixed train of pulses without reading the cell back. It is cheap, but device variation lands directly in the stored weight.
Oxygen vacancy
A missing oxygen atom in the oxide. Vacancies conduct better than the surrounding oxide, so a chain of them through the film is the conductive filament. A SET pulse moves oxygen ions and lines the vacancies up, and a RESET pulse refills them.
Program-and-verify (write-verify)
Writing in a loop: pulse, read back, compare with the target, and repeat until the cell is within tolerance or the pulses run out.
Quantization
Rounding to one of a limited set of values: inputs by the DAC, weights to conductance levels, outputs by the ADC.
Read disturb
A read at too high a voltage nudges the stored state. The read voltage is kept low to avoid it. Step 1 shows it.
RESET
A pulse that lowers a cell's conductance by dissolving part of the filament. In this model the block erase is a strong RESET.
SET
A pulse that raises a cell's conductance by building or widening the filament.
Sneak path
In a passive crossbar, current that finds a route through other cells instead of the selected one, which corrupts reads and writes. Transistors (1T1R) or selector devices suppress it. This simulator does not model sneak paths.
Tolerance
How close to the target counts as done in a verify loop, set as a percent of the Gmax − Gmin window.
Von Neumann bottleneck
The time and energy spent moving data between a separate memory and processor. Computing in the array avoids moving the weights.
Weight
A number the model learned. Here it runs from −1 to +1 and is stored as conductance in one or two cells.
Word line (WL)
The wire that runs along a row and carries one input voltage to every cell in that row.

No term matches. Try a shorter word.

What each setting does

Each row says what happens when you raise the setting. The numbers are the RMS output error (or the write cost) measured on the simulator's default 4 × 4 example with 10% variation and program-and-verify writing, changing one setting at a time. They are a guide to the direction and size of each effect, and will shift if you change other settings or the simulator is updated.

Common questions

Why store a weight as conductance and not resistance?

Because currents on a shared wire add in terms of conductance: I = V × (G₁ + G₂ + ...). Resistances in parallel combine through reciprocals, which does not give a sum. With conductance, the wire does the accumulation for free.

Why does a weight need two cells?

A conductance cannot be negative. One cell holds the positive part and one holds the negative part, and the read-out subtracts them. Step 3 walks through it.

Does reading a cell erase or change the weight?

Not at a small read voltage. The read stays inside the range where the cell acts like a plain resistor. A read at a high voltage can nudge the state, which is called read disturb (Step 1).

Is the crossbar analog or digital?

The array itself is analog: voltages go in, currents add, and a current comes out. The DACs and ADCs at the edges are the interface to the digital system around it.

If ReRAM is so efficient, why does not every chip use it?

Writing is slow and costly, cells vary and drift, endurance is limited, large arrays suffer IR drop and sneak paths, and the converters take energy and area. It suits cases where the same weights are read many times, such as running a trained network. The efficiency panel in Step 2 lets you see where the advantage appears and where it does not.

Why verify after each pulse?

The same pulse moves different cells by different amounts. Reading back lets the controller stop each cell near its target. In Step 3, switch to open loop and press Try a different cell to see the spread.

Is this simulator a model of a real chip?

No. It is a generic teaching model. The device response, the energies and the timings are illustrative values, and it has not been compared against measured hardware or a circuit simulator such as SPICE.
