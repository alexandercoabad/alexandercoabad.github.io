## AgilA32 browser simulator

The chip's Verilog (RV32I CPU and the two-state-machine PIO) compiled to WebAssembly and runs in the browser:
`agila32/index.html` (also available at `docs/sim/index.html` and `sim/index.html`) is a self-contained page with WebAssembly embedded.

Scenarios: a 7-segment display spelling "PILIPINAS AGILA32" (the default, source in `pilipinas_agila.S`, 7 instructions plus a 16-byte table), the same program with "PILIPINAS LASALLE" (`pilipinas.S`), a 7-segment display looping "AGILA32" (`agila_7seg.S`), boot ROM demo, boot ROM without a QSPI Pmod, the PIO signature test, the PIO smoke test, and your own program over the GPIO bootloader.

Verification: the signature scenario reproduces all 20 golden `uo_out` values and the pad sequence of `test/test_pio_gl_sig.py`;
the smoke scenario passes the same checks as `test/test_pio_gl.py`.

Note: the PIO block is an independent implementation of the RP2040 PIO instruction set, not affiliated with or endorsed by Raspberry Pi Ltd; Raspberry Pi, RP2040 and Pico are Raspberry Pi's trademarks. The Pico SDK programs used by the AgilA32 repository are listed in its `NOTICE.md`.

Rebuild: `tools/build_wasm_sim.sh` (yosys CXXRTL, then `zig c++` to wasm32-wasi). `sim.cpp` is the wrapper (clock stepping, flash and PSRAM models, trace); `template.html` is the page source. Sources are also collected in `sources/agila32-simulator/`.

## MC14500B browser simulator

`mc14500b/index.html` is a hand-written JavaScript model of `tt_um_mc14500b_soc_extended` (the 1-bit MC14500B-style SoC): 16-opcode ISA, 64-byte program RAM, free-running 6-bit PC, 8 scratch bits, edge detector (address 8), clock divider (address 9), 8-bit output shift latch (address C), and the input taps (D/E/F). It follows `docs/ISA.md` of the MC14500B_IHP repository, including its single-execution-per-instruction timing under the clock divider. There is no WebAssembly step: the page is plain HTML and JavaScript with no build.

Examples (each one has an editable assembler listing, a chip clock menu and a program RAM view with the PC highlighted):

- Kill the Bit (Altair classic), with an external monitor that stops the chip clock like the RP2040 firmware does
- Traffic light (two-way plus pedestrian crossing)
- Elevator (3 floors, E-stop)
- Boolean gates on switches
- Button toggle (edge detector)
- 7-segment display: PILIPINASLASALLE in a loop (5 kHz default, blinking decimal point)
- 7-segment 0-9 counter
- Blink (clock divider)

Controls: BTN is `ui_in[0]` (hold, or press Space), and the checkboxes are `ui_in[5]`, `ui_in[6]` and `ui_in[7]`. The 7-segment programs were produced with a search for the shortest instruction chain per segment; the generator code is in the page source (`johnsonDisplay`, `fsrDisplay`).

Not verified against the Verilog in a simulator: the page was checked by running the examples and comparing their output sequences, not by co-simulation with `project.v`.

## ReRAM crossbar simulator

`reram/crossbar/index.html` is a generic, textbook model of analog ReRAM compute-in-memory. It is plain HTML and JavaScript with no build. Weights are stored as differential conductance pairs (G+ and G-) in a passive crossbar, inputs are applied as word-line voltages, and each bit line sums its cell currents (Ohm's law per cell, Kirchhoff's current law per column). The pair is subtracted and digitized by an ADC, then compared with the ideal floating-point product.

Three-step lesson: `reram/basics/index.html` is Step 1, meant to be read before the write walkthrough (Step 2) and the crossbar simulator (Step 3). It compares a ReRAM cell with an ordinary resistor: a cell you can SET, RESET, read (including read disturb) and power off, an I-V loop with a read-range band, a variation, drift and endurance demo, a first section that is one live cell figure: a pulse generator wired across the electrodes, oxygen-ion dots, the O²⁻ arrow and a dashed outline of the filament before the last pulse, with the SET/RESET polarity explained under it (SET and RESET are opposite-polarity pulses across the top and bottom electrodes; the SET, RESET, Read, Erase and power buttons and the amplitude slider drive it, and the pulse is checked against the 0.8 V threshold), a second section with a seven-rung ladder of filament states showing how weights are dialled in pulse by pulse, and how the pulse is injected in a 1T1R cell and in a passive crossbar with the V/2 scheme, warning when the half-selected cells would be disturbed), a comparison table, a preview of what each difference means in the simulator, and six check-yourself questions. 

`reram/write/index.html` (Step 2) follows one weight from a number to a conductance in six steps: start with a weight, pick the cell (differential pair), round to a level, erase, pulse-read-compare until close enough (with a step-by-step log, waveform and trajectory charts), and the result with time, energy and accuracy. Sliders set bits, tolerance, start voltage, voltage step, pulse limit and device variation.

Below the six steps, `reram/write/` has a wiring panel, "How a pulse physically reaches one cell in the array". It answers the question of how many connections a cell has: two terminals (top and bottom electrode), with SET, RESET and read all using the same two and differing only in polarity and amplitude. A passive crossbar has two kinds of lines (word lines to the top electrodes, bit lines to the bottom electrodes), and a 1T1R array has three (word line to the transistor gate, bit line, source line). It starts with a one-cell schematic (word line on the top electrode, bit line on the bottom electrode) showing the voltages and current direction for SET, RESET and read on the same two wires, with the selected operation highlighted. The rest of the panel is an interactive 3 x 3 array: choose SET, RESET, read or block erase, passive (V/2 scheme, with a warning when the half-selected cells would see more than the 0.8 V threshold) or 1T1R, a pulse amplitude and a cell (click it or use the menus), and the drawing, a text explanation and a table show the voltage on every line. Four short FAQ entries cover the "SET line and RESET line", the read line, how the G+ and G- cells of a weight share a word line, and how many wires a bigger array needs. Steps 4 and 5 link to it. It is a biasing schematic, not a circuit model.

`reram/reference/index.html` is the study sheet for all three steps: a learning path, the signal chain, eleven equations with worked examples, a filterable glossary, a symbols and units table, what each simulator setting does, FAQ and further reading. All four pages and the home page link to each other.

Page order in the simulator: the crossbar and its five-step walkthrough come first, then the three examples, then the controls, the write panel, the results and the efficiency panel, then the references.

Cell encoding: a drop-down in the array-size panel switches between two layouts. *Analog multi-level* is the default described above. *Binary weighted ReRAM (1-bit cells)* turns each weight into a sign plus a B-bit magnitude (B is the Weight levels slider). Every bit goes into its own G+/G- pair of binary cells (Gmin = 0, Gmax = 1) on its own bit lines, each bit plane is read by its own ADC, and the digital side shifts each plane by 2^b and adds them. It uses B times as many cells and ADC conversions, but each cell only needs two well-separated states. The crossbar view, write panel, trace table, walkthrough, writing walkthrough (Example 2) and efficiency model follow the selected encoding. Examples 1 to 3 run in the analog layout, so loading one switches the drop-downs back to it.

Warm-up (first panel after the crossbar): one ReRAM cell as a resistor with a programmable conductance. Sliders for row voltage V and conductance G show I = V x G with R = 1/G and a water-pipe picture (pressure, pipe width, flow); a drop-down adds a second cell on the same bit line so the currents add (Kirchhoff) and the sum V1 G1 + V2 G2 appears as a dot product. The default values are exactly the G+1 bit line of the worked example below. The G slider range follows Gmin and Gmax.

Worked example (next panel): a real 2x2 vector multiply, y = x^T W, with every number visible. The student edits a 2-element input vector and a 2x2 weight matrix and sees, in five cards, how the inputs become voltages (DAC rounding and the read voltage), how the weights become conductances (rounding to a level, then one cell in G+ for a positive weight or G- for a negative one), the current of every cell (Ohm's law) and of every bit line (Kirchhoff), the subtraction, scaling and ADC step, and finally the simulated result against the ideal float answer. A table shows the value after each stage (ideal, after rounding x and W, analog crossbar, after the ADC) so the source of any error is visible, and the page names the largest contributor. A Devices selector switches between ideal cells and wires and the variation sigma and wire resistance from the Device and circuit panel, and a button loads the same numbers into the main crossbar above. Directly under the diagram a Live computation panel traces the whole calculation with the current numbers and recomputes on every change to an input, a weight or any Device and circuit setting (DAC, weight and ADC bits, ADC range, Vread, Gmin, Gmax, sigma, wire resistance): the DAC step (x, times the number of levels, rounded level, voltage), the weight step (|w| times the levels, rounded level, conductance, plus variation when Devices is on), and for each output the cell-current sums, the subtraction, the scaling, the ADC step size, unrounded value, code and digital y, and a check against the exact arithmetic. The panel is two columns wide (DAC and weights on top, y1 and y2 side by side below; one column on phones). Values that changed flash briefly (a static highlight with reduced motion) and a line names the last change, e.g. "ADC bits 8 → 4". Inside the diagram, each cell box shows its conductance G in µS (big number) and its current I in µA (small number), both written with their units (the main crossbar above also prints µS or µA under the number in each cell, following the Cell label menu); a key line explains this, and tapping (or pressing Enter on) any cell opens a "Reading one cell" box that decodes it with the current numbers: R = 1/G, the weight, level and G formula (or why an unused half of a pair sits at Gmin), the row voltage and I = V × G, any device-variation or IR-drop difference, a "Why not 0 µS?" note (the high-resistance state is not a true off, so Gmin is the best zero a cell offers and the G+/G- subtraction cancels it), a unit-check line showing why volts times µS gives µA (G in 10⁻⁶ S, and the same result from R = 1/G and I = V/R), and the cell's share of its bit-line total. A Cell layout drop-down in the worked example compares three ways to store the weights: *Differential pair* (default, 2 cells per weight, G+ for positive and G- for negative weights, the Gmin leakage cancels in the subtraction), *G+ only, negatives clipped* (1 cell per weight, negative weights become 0 and the Gmin leakage is not cancelled, so y is visibly wrong), and *G+ only + reference column* (1 cell per weight plus one shared reference column at the middle conductance; weights are shifted as u = (w+1)/2, the reference current is subtracted from every output, and the result is divided by Vread x (Gmax - Gmin)/2; it handles negatives and cancels the leakage but each step in w is twice as coarse, and 0 is only exactly representable when the level count is odd). The diagram, cards, Live computation panel and cell explanation all follow the selected layout. The Load into the crossbar button is available only for the differential layout, because the main crossbar uses it. Layout: on screens 1000 px and wider the top of the worked example is two columns (inputs, buttons and the matrix-to-cell note on the left, the diagram on the right), the predict questions run in two columns from 1100 px, and the result cards fill a three-column grid with cards 4 and 5 sharing the last row; narrower screens stack everything in one column.

Matrix-to-cell highlighting: hovering or focusing a weight in W rings its cell or cells in the diagram (both cells of the pair, one cell, or the cell plus the reference column, depending on the layout), dims the rest, and a note under the diagram says where the weight is stored, its conductance and which row voltage drives it. Hovering an input x highlights its whole row, and hovering or selecting a cell highlights its weight. Predict first: a collapsible panel under the diagram asks five questions (what happens to y1 when x2 falls, which cell passes the most current, a 2-bit DAC, a 4-bit ADC, clipped negatives); the student picks an answer, sees a short explanation and a score, and a button resets the default example, applies the change and shows it in the diagram and Live computation. The expected answers were checked against the simulator's own output. It uses the analog multi-level layout.

Layout: the Input vector, Weight matrix and Device and circuit panels now sit in a left column beside the Crossbar diagram, so students can change a value and watch the array respond without scrolling. On a narrow screen the crossbar comes first and the controls follow it. The two-column option rows in the Programming and Efficiency panels were widened to fit the full-width layout.

Weight sign and cells per weight: two drop-downs next to Cell encoding answer a question every ReRAM design faces, because a conductance, and so a current, is never negative. *Weight sign* chooses whether negative weights are kept (*Signed*) or stored as 0 (*Unsigned*). *Cells per weight* chooses a *G+/G- pair* (two cells, currents subtracted) or *G+ only* (one cell). The valid combinations are three: Signed with a pair (the default), Signed with G+ only (the weight is shifted into 0 to 1 by u = (w+1)/2 and one shared reference column at mid-conductance is subtracted from every output, gain 2; half the cells but each weight step is twice as coarse), and Unsigned with G+ only (negatives set to 0, no subtraction; negative weights turn red, a "Make weights positive" button appears and the Gmin leakage adds an offset to every output). Unsigned with a pair is not offered because G- would sit at Gmin and add nothing; choosing Unsigned switches Cells per weight to G+ only. A discussion panel under the array controls ("Negative weights: signed or unsigned, one cell or two?") explains the two choices, compares the three layouts in a table that highlights the current one, answers three common student questions and lists four things to try. All three combine with both cell encodings (six combinations in total). The write walkthrough, trace table, step text, efficiency model and the "Load into the crossbar" button of the 2x2 worked example follow the selection, and loading the worked example sets the same layout in the main crossbar. The reference page has an equation card (5b), glossary entries and control-table rows for the same options. Examples 1 to 3 run in the signed differential analog layout.

Example 1 (below the crossbar): this panel is a tiny neural-network layer that answers "which side of a picture is lit?" A dropdown picks a 2x2 picture (4 pixels) or a 3x3 patch (9 pixels). The four outputs are Left, Right, Top and Bottom templates stored as weights of +1 (want lit), -1 (want dark) and, in the 3x3 case, 0 (don't care). The 3x3 templates are Sobel-style edge kernels, so the panel doubles as one step of a convolution in image analysis. Students tap pixels or pick a preset and see each template's sum worked out by hand next to the crossbar's actual ADC reading. "Load this example into the simulator" fills the array with those weights (a 4 x 4 array for 2x2, a 9 x 4 array for 3x3, ideal devices so the sums are exact), and "Watch the crossbar compute it" plays the five-step walkthrough. Changing the picture size while the example is loaded reloads the simulator at the new size. A corner ties two templates and a diagonal matches none, which makes good discussion questions. Raising the variation or the wire resistance afterwards shows how noise can flip the winner. Before it is loaded the panel previews the scores with plain arithmetic and does not touch the simulator.

Example 2, writing walkthrough: the next panel shows, step by step, how one weight from the simulator's matrix gets stored in a pair of ReRAM cells. Students tap any entry of the matrix and step through (Back, Next or Play): the weight, why it needs a G+ and a G- cell, rounding to one of the available conductance levels and the resulting target in microsiemens, the erase, and then the program-and-verify loop of pulse, read back, compare and repeat. Two conductance gauges and a pulse-by-pulse chart show the cell creeping up on its target band, and the final step reads the stored weight back and compares it with the wanted one. It uses the same write model and the same per-cell random seeds as the Programming panel, so the same cell gets exactly the same pulses in both places (with Write method set to Program-and-verify). A variation slider shows that identical targets need different numbers of pulses on different cells. The detailed circuit, waveform and whole-array cost stay in the Programming panel further down.

Example 3, XOR: the next panel shows why one crossbar is not enough. A single crossbar computes only a weighted sum, so it can separate the four input cases of XOR with one straight line at best, which scores 3 of 4 (the default weights make an OR gate). The default two-crossbar network adds a hidden layer: crossbar A computes h1 (at least one input on) and h2 (both on), a comparator turns each sum into a 0 or 1, and crossbar B computes y = h1 - h2 - 0.5. Students flip x1 and x2 and watch the sums and bits move through both arrays, read a truth table with a score of correct cases, and switch to the one-crossbar mode to see it stop at 3 of 4. A collapsed "Experiment" section holds the weight editor and the noise controls for going deeper. Variation, wire resistance, DAC/ADC bits, weight levels, Gmin, Gmax and read voltage all apply, so raising the variation (and resampling) eventually flips a case. This panel has its own small crossbar model that reuses the same solver, with ideal weight writes (no programming model) and a fixed set of device-variation samples tied to the seed.

References: the last panel on the page lists 14 sources for students, grouped by the part of the simulator they support (memristor and ReRAM devices, crossbar vector-matrix multiplication, writing the weights, neural networks on ReRAM hardware, ReRAM in silicon with SKY130, and splitting weights across several cells). Each entry has a one-line note on why it is worth reading and, where available, a DOI and a free version. Citation details were checked against publisher or index records on 1 November 2026. The SKY130 group is tools and announcements rather than papers, and the shuttle article it lists describes a submission window that has closed, so check the current schedule. To add a source, copy one of the list items in the section with id "refs" and keep the numbering in the "start" attribute of each list continuous.

Controls: array size (2 to 9 rows, 2 to 6 columns), input vector, weight matrix, DAC, weight-level and ADC bit depths, ADC range, read voltage, Gmin and Gmax, Gaussian device variation and wire resistance. Wire resistance is solved as a full resistive network (Gauss-Seidel), so IR drop appears in the outputs. A five-step walkthrough (program, drive rows, Ohm's law, sum columns, digitize) animates the crossbar, and one output column can be traced cell by cell.

The write panel models how the weights get into the array. A controller block-erases the cells to the high-resistance state, then writes each one with SET and RESET pulses. In the default program-and-verify mode (incremental step pulse programming) it reads the cell back after every pulse, raises the amplitude by a fixed step while it undershoots, and restarts from the low amplitude when a pulse overshoots. An open-loop mode replays a pulse train calibrated on a nominal device with no read-back, and an ideal mode skips programming. The page draws the write circuitry (pulse generator and DAC, row decoder, bit-line drivers with a current limit, sense amplifier and ADC, comparator, state machine), the loop as a flowchart, the voltage waveform and conductance trajectory of any chosen cell, a per-cell pulse-count map, and the total write time and energy. The conductances produced by the writes are the ones the crossbar then computes with. The cell response (an exponential approach to Gmax and Gmin with per-device strength and pulse-to-pulse noise) and the write energies are illustrative, not fitted to a real device.

The efficiency panel compares the crossbar with a digital multiply-accumulate (MAC) processor for latency and energy at the current size, at 256 x 256, and across array sizes from 4 x 4 to 512 x 512. Array energy comes from the simulated currents (V x I x read pulse). The digital MAC energy, memory fetch energy, DAC and ADC energies and timings are illustrative ballpark values that you can edit, not measurements of any chip. Programming is included as a one-time cost spread over a reuse count you choose, with an energy break-even point. The model leaves out sneak paths, routing and digital post-processing, so small arrays can favor digital.

This is a teaching model, not a novel architecture and not a model of a specific chip. It has not been compared against measured ReRAM hardware or a circuit simulator such as SPICE.

## PCM lessons

`pcm/basics/` compares PCM with ReRAM (rather than with an ordinary resistor, as the ReRAM basics page does): both cells get the same pulses, the I-V plot overlays the PCM snap and the ReRAM SET half, and the strip chart shows both technologies' variation, drift and wear. The ReRAM cell model is the one from `reram/basics/`. The PCM series mirrors the ReRAM one: `pcm/basics/` (Step 1: PCM vs ReRAM), `pcm/write/` (Step 2: RESET, SET staircase with program-and-verify, overshoot restart, and drift after the write), `pcm/crossbar/` (Step 3, the crossbar simulator; `pcm/index.html` is the overview page) and `pcm/reference/`. All five PCM pages are complete. `pcm/crossbar/index.html` is the simulator. It is the ReRAM crossbar page without the ReRAM-specific tutorial sections (warm-up, worked 2×2 example, extension examples), with the PCM write model from Step 2 (RESET, adaptive SET pulses, overshoot restart) and two added settings: time since write (power-law drift, ν = 0.005 + 0.095·a) and a global drift gain. `pcm/reference/` is the PCM counterpart of `reram/reference/`: same layout and searchable glossary, with the cell equations rewritten (dome fraction, temperature-driven SET and RESET, adaptive write loop, drift) and the settings table re-measured on the PCM simulator. If the PCM simulator changes, re-measure the numbers in its "What each setting does" tables.

The ReRAM and PCM series are cross-linked page for page: each ReRAM page (`reram/basics/`, `reram/write/`, `reram/crossbar/` for the crossbar, `reram/reference/`) has a "Compare with PCM" link to its PCM counterpart (`pcm/basics/`, `pcm/write/`, `pcm/crossbar/`, `pcm/reference/`), and the PCM pages link back. Keep both directions when adding or moving a page.

## CAN bus lessons

The three CAN pages are self-contained and share one small simulation core, `var CAN=(function(){...})()`, which is pasted verbatim into each page (there is no build step, so keep the three copies identical when you change it). It covers frame layout, CRC-15, bit stuffing, arbitration keys, the Davis et al. response-time analysis and a discrete-event bus simulation (`CAN.simulate`).

Sensor samples (used by Step 3): a message can carry a `sensor` spec (`min`, `max`, `scale`, `offset`, `bytes`, `signed`) and a `signal(tUs)` function. The value is sampled when the message is released, scaled and packed little-endian into the data bytes (`CAN.packSample` / `CAN.unpackSample`), and every delivered frame is recorded in `result.rx[i]` as `{t, s, d}` (end of frame, sample instant, data bytes), so a receiver can decode what actually crossed the bus and work out how old it is (`CAN.lastRx` finds the latest delivery at a given time). Extra `simulate` options: `mailbox` (one transmit buffer per message, a newer sample replaces an unsent older one and is counted in `stats[i].over`), per-message `startMs` / `stopMs` (extra traffic that begins and ends), `errFromMs` / `errToMs` (the window in which noise can hit frames) and `busOffMs` (how long a bus-off node stays silent). Without these options `simulate` behaves exactly as before.

The reference page (`can/reference/index.html`) is static HTML and does not contain the simulation core. Its worked examples and the measured effects in its settings table were produced by running the core (500 kbit/s, 16 s run, noise seed 7), so re-check them if you change the core.

Lesson design (all three CAN pages): each page opens with three learning objectives and ends with a three-line summary. Step 1 and Step 3 have a prediction prompt, and Steps 1, 2 and 3 each have a hand calculation that checks the student's number against the model (longest bus length, frame length in bits and microseconds, bus load). `can/pilot-sheet.md` is a one-page observation sheet for trying the lessons with students.

Hover definitions: a term with a dotted underline (DLC, RTR, SRR, TEC, CRC, arbitration and so on) shows its glossary definition on hover or focus and links to its anchor on the reference page (`can/reference/#g-...`, highlighted on arrival). The text comes from the `GLOSS` table, which is pasted verbatim into Steps 1, 2 and 3, so keep the three copies identical; each entry's last item is the glossary anchor and must match an `id="g-..."` on the reference page. Step 2 links terms automatically (`autoGT` links the first occurrence of each term in a step's text); Steps 1 and 3 place them by hand with `GT(key)`.

Step 2 (`can/frame/`): a strip above each step's figure draws the whole frame as one bar and highlights the part that step works on (it follows the identifier, data and bit rate you chose in step 1). The arbitration step is drawn as a logic-analyser view: one trace per node, the bus as their AND, a time axis in microseconds at the chosen bit rate, the 75% sample point of each bit and a running "bus read" line. Step 4 holds the frame-length hand calculation.

Step 3 (`can/bus/`): six guided experiments (predict, run, "show what to expect"). Every figure in the "what to expect" text was checked against the simulation core; re-check them if you change the core or the default settings. Experiment 3 uses 150% extra traffic because at exactly 100% all six readings arrive late rather than lost.

## Adding another project

Create a folder with its own `index.html`, add a section to the root `index.html`, and add a section here.
