CAN bus vs separate wires

A modern car has dozens of small computers (ECUs) that all need to talk to each other: the engine, the brakes, the doors, the dashboard. Running a private wire between every pair would be a harness nobody could build. A Controller Area Network (CAN) puts all of them on one twisted pair of wires. Anyone can talk, everyone listens, and a simple rule decides who goes first when two talk at once. If you did the ReRAM and PCM lessons, the idea of a shared wire will feel familiar, but the physics is different: there, a shared bit line added currents. Here, a shared bus combines voltage levels with a logical AND. This page builds the bus, shows why two wires beat one, and shows what limits its speed and length, before Step 2 follows one message onto the bus.

What a CAN bus is

Every node (an ECU with a CAN controller and a transceiver) connects to the same two wires, called CAN_H and CAN_L. Nothing on the bus says “this message is for node 5”. Instead, every message carries an identifier that says what the message is (for example “engine speed”), and every node decides for itself whether it cares. At the two ends of the wire sit 120 Ω terminating resistors. A node can put one of two states on the bus. In the dominant state it drives the wires apart (logic 0). In the recessive state it lets go of them (logic 1). If one node is dominant and another is recessive, dominant wins. That single rule is what makes a shared wire work, and it is the basis of arbitration in Step 2.

Think of a meeting room

Everyone can hear everyone. Anyone can speak when the room is quiet. If two people start together, each keeps listening to their own voice against the room. The one who hears something different from what they said stops and waits. Nobody has to shout over the other, and the first speaker is not interrupted. CAN does exactly this, one bit at a time.

Think of a shared rope

A rope lies slack across a room. Any person may pull it taut. If nobody pulls, the rope is slack (recessive, 1). If one person or ten pull, the rope is taut (dominant, 0). You cannot tell how many people pulled, only that at least one did. Slack is the default, and pulling always wins. That is a wired AND of everyone’s bits.

Why cars care

With point-to-point links, 20 ECUs need up to 190 links (n(n−1)/2). On a bus they need 20 short stubs onto one pair of wires. That saves weight, cost and connectors, and a new ECU can be added without touching the others. The price is that all of them share the same capacity, so who goes first has to be decided.

Why it is called CAN, and what it is used for

Why “CAN”?

Controller Area Network: a network of controllers (small computers) in one local area, such as a car. It was developed by Bosch in the 1980s for road vehicles and became an international standard (ISO 11898).

It is message-based, not address-based, and multi-master: any node may start a message.

Where you meet it

Cars and trucks first, then everything else that needs many small, reliable, cheap links: factory machinery, lifts, medical equipment, ships, agricultural machines, robots and the diagnostic port (OBD-II) under a car dashboard.

It is built for short messages that arrive on time and for surviving a noisy electrical environment, not for moving large files.

As a design choice

Compare with a point-to-point UART link: no arbitration needed, but one cable per pair. Compare with SPI: very fast, but one master and one chip-select wire per slave. CAN trades speed (at most 1 Mbit/s in the classical version) for many nodes, two wires, built-in priorities and built-in error checking.

Section 5 puts the numbers side by side.

This page is about classical CAN (CAN 2.0A with 11-bit and 2.0B with 29-bit identifiers). CAN FD, which sends the data part faster and longer, is covered in the table in section 5 but is not simulated.

1. Wire up the bus

Four nodes share one bus. Each node can drive dominant (pull the wires apart, logic 0) or stay recessive (let go, logic 1). Press the buttons and watch the two wires. Nobody ever drives the bus to recessive: recessive is simply what is left when nobody is pulling. Then try the fault button: one node stuck in the dominant state silences every other node.

Levels on the wires (high-speed CAN, ISO 11898-2)

Recessive: both wires sit at about 2.5 V, so the difference is about 0 V. Dominant: CAN_H goes up to about 3.5 V and CAN_L down to about 1.5 V, so the difference is about 2 V. A receiver calls anything above about 0.9 V dominant and anything below about 0.5 V recessive, and the gap between 0.5 and 0.9 V gives some margin.

Every node reads back what is on the bus while it transmits. If it sent a 1 and reads a 0, somebody else is dominant. That mismatch is not an error during the identifier: it is how arbitration works (Step 2). Anywhere else it is a bit error.

2. Two wires and noise

Why two wires, and why twisted? A car is electrically harsh: ignition pulses, motors, relays and a ground that is not the same voltage everywhere. Noise that hits both wires equally (called common-mode noise) moves CAN_H and CAN_L together, so their difference does not change. The receiver only looks at the difference. A single wire against ground has no such protection. Below, the same 16 bits are sent two ways with the same noise. Raise the noise and compare. Twisting the pair makes the noise arrive nearly equal on both wires, which is why the imbalance can be small in practice. All numbers are illustrative.

3. Bits on the wire

There is no clock wire. Every node has its own oscillator and slices each bit into small pieces called time quanta. A bit has a synchronisation segment, a propagation segment, and two phase segments, and the receiver reads the level at the sample point. The sample point has to come after the signal has travelled to the farthest node and back, because a transmitting node checks the bus while it sends (section 1). That is why a faster bit rate means a shorter bus.

No clock wire, so the data must keep making edges: bit stuffing

A receiver keeps its clock in step by watching for edges. A long run of identical bits has no edges, so the sender inserts an opposite bit after every 5 equal bits (bit stuffing), and the receiver removes it. The stuffed bit counts as the first bit of the next run. Stuff bits are shown in orange below. Step 2 stuffs a real frame.

The price: up to one extra bit in five on the wire. Stuffing covers the frame from the start bit to the end of the CRC, but not the last few fixed bits (Step 2).

4. Why it is not perfect

CAN is robust and cheap, and it gives every message a priority, but it pays for that in several ways. Overhead: a frame carries at most 8 data bytes but about 47 to 67 other bits. Stuffing: the length of a frame depends on its content. Wiring: the bus needs a terminator at both ends and short stubs. Waiting: a low-priority message can be held up by every higher-priority one (Step 3).

8

Payload efficiency = 8n / bits in the frame (including the 3-bit gap between frames). Worst-case stuffing is 8n + 47 + ⌊(34 + 8n − 1)/4⌋ bits for a standard frame, and 8n + 67 + ⌊(54 + 8n − 1)/4⌋ for an extended one. With 8 bytes that is 135 and 160 bits. The tiles use the bit rate chosen in section 3.

Termination: why the ends matter

A fast edge travelling down a wire is partly reflected wherever the wire’s impedance changes, such as an open end. Reflections come back as ringing after the edge. With a 120 Ω resistor at both ends, which matches the cable, almost nothing reflects. With none, the ringing can cross the receiver’s thresholds and look like extra edges.

Illustrative: the ringing is a damped sine of 0%, 35% or 80% of the step for two, one or no terminators. Real ringing depends on cable length, stubs and the transceiver’s edge speed. A quick field check is to measure the resistance between CAN_H and CAN_L with the bus powered down: two 120 Ω terminators in parallel read about 60 Ω.

7. Check yourself

Two nodes transmit at the same moment. One sends a 1 and the other sends a 0. What is on the bus, and what do the two nodes read?

The bus is 0 (dominant), because dominant wins. Both nodes read 0. The node that sent 0 sees what it sent and carries on. The node that sent 1 sees a 0 and knows another node is transmitting with a higher priority at this bit, so it stops sending and becomes a receiver. Nothing is corrupted, which is why this is called non-destructive arbitration (Step 2).

Why is the dominant state called “0” and the recessive state “1”, and why does a lower identifier win?

On the wires, dominant means the node actively drives the pair apart, and it overrides anyone who is not driving. Calling it logic 0 makes the bus a wired AND. An identifier is sent most significant bit first, so the first bit where two identifiers differ is a 0 for the smaller number and a 1 for the larger one. The 0 is dominant, so the smaller number wins.

Why does a node read the bus while it is sending?

For two reasons. During the identifier, it lets the node notice that someone with a higher priority is also sending. After that, a mismatch means the bus does not carry what the node sent, so the frame is damaged and the node signals a bit error. Reading back is also why the sample point has to be later than the round-trip delay: the node has to see its own bit travel to the far end and come back.

A noise spike adds 3 V to both wires at once. What happens on a single wire against ground, and on a CAN pair?

A single wire is read against a fixed 2.5 V threshold, so a 0 can jump above it and be read as a 1. On a CAN pair, both wires rise by 3 V, so their difference is unchanged and the bit is read correctly. The pair is still safe as long as the common-mode voltage stays inside the receiver’s input range (about −2 V to +7 V). Try it in section 2.

You double the bit rate. What happens to the longest bus you can build, and why?

It shrinks to a little under half. The bit time halves, so the round trip must fit in half the time before the sample point. Part of that time is the fixed delay of the node electronics, so the length falls by slightly more than half (in the model: 130 m at 500 kbit/s and 50 m at 1 Mbit/s, with an 80% sample point and 150 ns of node delay). Section 3 lets you check this.

Why does the sender insert a bit after five equal bits, and does that mean a frame has a fixed length?

The bus has no clock wire, so receivers need edges to stay in step. Stuffing guarantees an edge at least every six bits. The number of stuff bits depends on the data, so the same frame with different payloads has different lengths. A standard frame with 8 data bytes is 111 bits with no stuffing and at most 135 bits including the 24 worst-case stuff bits.

The bus is dead and every node reports errors. Name two physical things you could check first.

Whether a node is stuck dominant (try disconnecting nodes one at a time and watch the bus idle at about 2.5 V on both wires), and whether the terminators are right (about 60 Ω between CAN_H and CAN_L with the power off: 120 Ω means one is missing, and an open circuit means both are). Short circuits between the wires or to ground, and a mismatched bit rate on one node, are also common.

Why can a CAN node send only 8 data bytes, and is that a problem for a camera or a firmware update?

The classical frame was designed for small control messages that must arrive quickly and predictably, and a short frame also limits how long a high-priority message can be blocked. A camera stream or a firmware image has to be split into many frames by a higher-layer protocol, and the overhead in section 4 means only about half the raw bit rate carries data. CAN FD raises the payload to 64 bytes for that reason, and Ethernet is used where large data is the main job.
